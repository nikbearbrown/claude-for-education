# Introduction: Mentorship as a Design Problem

This guide is for PhD students and postdocs who have been assigned their first undergraduate intern and want to do the job well. Mentoring an undergraduate researcher is a skill, and like any skill, it can be learned. This course breaks it down into concrete practices, organized around the specific problems each practice is designed to prevent.

The central idea is that undergraduate research failure is a mentor design problem, not a student competence problem. The most common ways these internships go wrong — mismatched expectations, technical drift, stalled identity development, a disorganized handover, quiet exclusion, and prolonged dependence — each results from a missing structure, not a deficient student. Because each failure has a specific cause, each has a fix that can be put in place in advance.

This reframing matters because the natural instinct points in the other direction. When an intern fails a protocol repeatedly, it is easy to assume she is not grasping the technique. When she stops asking questions, it can seem like disengagement. This course trains you to read those same signals as diagnostic information about your own supervision approach — and to respond accordingly. Supervision is a technical skill. It can be specified, practiced, and refined like any other protocol in your lab.

### Who This Is For

This course is designed for PhD students and postdocs with real domain expertise and no formal training in mentorship. If you have just been assigned your first undergraduate intern and want to mentor effectively without it taking over your year, this course is for you. It can be completed in a single sitting before the intern's first day, and individual modules can be revisited as each stage of the internship arrives.

### How This Course Is Organized

The six modules follow the intern's lifecycle. Each module addresses a specific failure mode.

**Module 1 — Establishing the Mentorship Compact** addresses the Day-1 failure of expectation mismatch. You will design a written compact specifying timeline, communication, and documentation before the intern arrives.

**Module 2 — Scaffolding Technical Competence** addresses the Week 1–8 failure of protocol drift. You will execute the three-stage Modeling → Scaffolding → Fading apprenticeship and learn to diagnose why a protocol keeps failing.

**Module 3 — Cultivating Research Identity** addresses the Month 2–4 failure of identity stall. You will use psychological safety and intellectual inclusion to move a mentee from lab hand to researcher.

**Module 4 — Transitioning to Independence and Exit** addresses the Month 5–6 failure of the botched handover. You will run a structured exit interview and a clean documentation handover. This module ends with the summative exam required for course completion.

**Module 5 — Establishing a Practice of Inclusion** and **Module 6 — Fostering Independence** are cross-arc practices. They do not belong to a single stage; they apply across the whole internship and across every intern you will ever mentor. Module 5 trains you to recognize and respond to exclusion; Module 6 trains you to define independence benchmarks and exit your own role as the bottleneck.

### How to Use This Course

Read Modules 1–4 in order — each builds on the last, and Module 4's exam assumes familiarity with all of them. Treat Modules 5 and 6 as the practices that separate a successful first internship from a mentorship approach you can repeat. Throughout, every concept is presented the same way: the failure it prevents, the principle, a lab-situated example, and a practice you can apply on your intern's next working day.

Begin with Module 1 and the Mentorship Compact.
