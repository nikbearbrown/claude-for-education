# Chapter 11 — Building Your Second Evidence Stream

*You do not need a platform to begin. You need to pick two components, add them to what you already do, and observe what you find.*

---

Everything in the book up to here has been argument. This chapter is different. It is shorter, more operational, and exists for one purpose: to close the gap between a framework you read about and a framework you used. By the end of it you should have two component choices written down and a first implementation sketched.

The full seven-component framework is the destination. One component is the starting point. Two is realistic. Three is ambitious for a first term and you should not start there. The biggest implementation failure is choosing components by theoretical importance rather than accessibility — picking the two that sound most diagnostic, discovering the workload isn't sustainable, and ending up with no data at all. A reader who picks two achievable components and accumulates two terms of useful data has done the right thing.

---

## Which components to start with

Not all seven cost the same to implement. Some require nothing beyond a question added to your existing quiz. Others require coded analysis of discussion transcripts. The right starting point is the most accessible component for your current infrastructure, not the most important one in the abstract.

Here is the accessibility ladder, from easiest to hardest.

**Y4 — Uncertainty Calibration.** Add one question after each quiz item: "How confident are you in this answer? Not confident / Somewhat confident / Very confident." Track the gap between confidence and accuracy over the term. Infrastructure required: a column in your gradebook. Time to first data: one quiz. This is the confidence-elicitation practice that Dylan Wiliam has been advocating since the 1990s — traffic-light cards, red-amber-green responses, the one-question confidence check. It has been deployed in thousands of classrooms. Nothing here is hypothetical.

**Y5-lite — Confusion Notes.** Ask students to submit one paragraph with each major assignment: "What confused you most about this material, and why?" You are not running the full SKT rubric. You are reading for texture: specific confusions, questions that could not have come from a summary, real engagement with the material's difficulty. Infrastructure required: a paragraph field on existing submissions. Time to first data: one assignment. This is the one-minute paper from Angelo and Cross's 1993 *Classroom Assessment Techniques*, in a form familiar to any practitioner who has done structured reflection prompts.

**Y6 — Retrieval Strength Decay.** Embed one or two questions from previous units into each new quiz, unannounced. Track each student's performance on these decay probes across the term. Infrastructure required: a question bank tagged by unit. Time to first data: three or four weeks. The curve either holds up or it collapses. Interpretive complexity is low once the data accumulates.

**Y3 — Cross-Context Transfer.** Design one near-transfer and one far-transfer item per major concept. Score them separately. Infrastructure required: thoughtful assessment design, no new tools. Time to first data: one assessment cycle. Interpretive complexity is moderate — the gap interpretation depends on the specific items.

**Y1 — Temporal Engagement Pattern.** Approximable by asking students to annotate problem sets with checkmarks and question marks, then comparing annotations to actual difficulty. Without an LMS that logs sub-paragraph time, the platform version of Y1 is out of reach. Infrastructure required: annotated assignments. Interpretive complexity: moderate.

**Y2 — Error Trajectory Coherence.** Implementable only after you have built or borrowed a misconception map for your topic. Coding errors by misconception type is real work. Infrastructure required: a misconception taxonomy. Interpretive complexity: high.

**Y7 — Scaffolding Response Curve.** Easy in any context with sustained individual contact — office hours, tutoring, clinical bedside teaching. Not easily implementable in large lectures with no individual contact. Infrastructure required: a two-level hint pair per concept, and the discipline to write down what level of hint you offered.

Most readers will find two of these components very accessible in their current practice. Pick those two.

## Starting points for three contexts

**High school science.** Start with Y4 and Y5-lite. Both fit inside existing assessment without LMS modification. Y4 is a one-question add-on to weekly quizzes. Y5-lite is a one-paragraph add-on to each major assignment. A high school science department chair could implement both across her department this term and have profiles for every student in every class by week six. Neither requires new infrastructure. Neither requires training. The data is generated inside assessment the students are already doing.

**Higher education, lecture-and-section format.** Start with Y6 and Y3. In a lecture course, Y4 and Y5-lite suffer from low response rates and self-selection bias — students who care enough to report confusion are not the students whose confusion you most need to see. Y6 is collected automatically inside graded quizzes, which students take because they count; the decay signal arrives without any new student-facing behavior change. Y3 is collected inside unit assessments for the same reason. Both are robust against social-desirability bias. Time to first data is three to four weeks, but the data arrives without changing the student experience at all.

**Clinical education.** Start with Y7 and Y5 (full rubric). Clinical education has continuous individual contact as its native format. Y7 is implementable inside existing bedside teaching with no infrastructure beyond writing down the hint level required. Y5 in full form is feasible because case presentations are verbal artifacts the attending is already evaluating — applying the SKT rubric is an explicit version of a judgment she is already making. Both live naturally inside what clinical educators already do.

The triage logic is the same across all three contexts: look at the accessibility ladder, pick the two components your current infrastructure already supports, start there.

## What the first term looks like

Weeks 1 and 2: choose your two components and build the small instrumentation. For Y4 this means adding the confidence question to your quiz template. For Y5-lite it means writing the confusion-note prompt. For Y6 it means tagging questions by unit. The instrumentation takes a few hours, not days. Resist the temptation to build elaborate systems. Elaborate systems get abandoned in week four.

Weeks 3 and 4: collect the first data. Do not analyze it yet. The signals are noisy early. Confidence calibration looks unremarkable in week three because students do not yet know what to expect of the material. Decay probes have no decay yet because the material is too recent. The data is not telling you anything reliable until you have at least three to four weeks of it. Sit with it.

Week 5: look at the data for the first time, at the class level, not the individual level. Class-level patterns are more reliable early. Note what you see — the distribution of confidence gaps, the range of confusion-note specificity, the shape of decay across the class. Do not act yet.

Weeks 6 to 8: begin individual-level reading. By week six you have three or four weeks of accumulated data per student. Now you can read profiles. Identify three or four students whose profile surprises you — students where the artifact grade and the process signals disagree. Have one conversation with each of them. The conversation is not "I think you're using AI." The conversation is "I noticed this pattern and wanted to understand what you're experiencing with the material." Take notes.

Weeks 9 to 11: refine your reading. By now you have a sense of what the signals are telling you in your specific course. One of your two components is probably more diagnostic than the other in your context. Consider adding a third component only if the first two have stabilized. Resist expanding too fast.

Weeks 12 to 14: end-of-term reflection. Look at profile-vs-artifact agreement and disagreement across the class. Compare end-of-term assessment results to the profile-based predictions you would have made at week six. The framework calibrates itself to your judgment across the first term. By the second term you will have a working sense of what each signal is worth in your specific classroom.

The most important rule about what to do with the data: it is for conversations with students, not for grade calculations. A first-term implementation that uses the data to flag students for outreach and support is doing the right thing. A first-term implementation that uses the data to adjust grades is doing the wrong thing. Wait at least two terms before considering any credentialing use of the data, and read the ethics section below before you do.

The single most reliable first-term failure mode is collapsing the two phases. Collect data this term. Act on it next term. That discipline is the most important implementation rule in the chapter.

## The ethics of process observation

The framework asks instructors to collect process data on students. That collection is a moral act, and the chapter cannot be honest without addressing it directly.

The distinction the framework rests on is this: observation for learning support is not the same activity as observation for surveillance, even when they collect the same data. The difference is purpose, data ownership, and what the data is used for. Observation for learning support has the student's learning as its purpose, makes the data available to the student, and is used to inform teaching in the student's service. Observation for surveillance has institutional accountability as its purpose, makes the data primarily available to actors above the student, and is used to make decisions about the student rather than for her.

Neil Selwyn's critique of learning analytics — his argument that good-intentioned process data tends to drift toward surveillance regardless of designer intent — is real. The drift is mitigable through deliberate design choices, not impossible. The chapter argues mitigation is possible. It does not pretend mitigation is automatic.

The default the framework recommends: the student has access to her own profile (transparency); the instructor has access to her students' profiles for teaching purposes (pedagogical use); the institution does not have access to individual-student profiles for grading, ranking, or disciplinary purposes (the surveillance boundary). Aggregate de-identified data at the institutional level is acceptable. Individual-level data flowing to administrators is the threshold where the framework's design envelope is being exited.

Three mitigations against the chilling effect — the risk that knowing she is being observed causes a student to perform rather than engage. First, transparency: tell students the framework exists, what it measures, what the data is used for, and what it is not used for. Students who understand the framework as learning support behave differently from students who experience it as surveillance, even with identical data collection. Second, low stakes: keep the data out of grading decisions for at least the first term. Third, student agency: invite students to view their own profiles and use them for self-regulation. Students who own their assessment data engage differently with the process than students for whom assessment is something done to them.

The bottom line: process observation is legitimate when its purpose is the student's learning, the student has access to the data, the data is not used adversarially, and the framework is transparent. It slides toward surveillance when any of those conditions fail. The distinction is in the design, not in the data.

## A worked example: a community college instructor's first semester

Maria teaches English at a community college. She has chosen two components for her first term: Y4 on weekly quizzes and Y5-lite as a paragraph submitted with each major assignment. She has fourteen weeks and twenty-six students.

She tells the class on week one what the additions are for: "I am collecting these because they help me understand what you are actually experiencing with the material, beyond what your essays alone tell me. I will use them to decide where to focus my teaching. They are not graded." That is the transparency move. It takes ninety seconds. It changes how students respond.

The first round of confidence ratings is uniformly high — typical week-three undergraduate behavior. The first round of confusion notes is uneven. About a third of students produce specific confusions ("I don't see why the narrator is unreliable in chapter three when she gives factual information that turns out to be true"). About a third produce generic statements ("It was mostly clear but a few things were hard"). The middle third is somewhere between. Maria does not analyze it yet.

By week six she has three quizzes of confidence data and three confusion notes per student. Patterns are emerging. Two students with strong essay grades have flatly generic confusion notes — no specific encounter with the material visible across three weeks of submissions. Three students with weaker essay grades have specific, textured confusions that suggest genuine engagement with the readings beyond what their essays are showing. She reaches out to all five for conversations. With the two strong-essay, generic-note students, she asks about their reading process — not adversarially, but as a genuine inquiry. She is gathering information. With the three weaker-essay, textured-note students, she invites them to office hours for draft revision, because the confusion notes suggest the underlying engagement is there and the essay is the bottleneck.

By end of term, the pattern holds. The students whose generic confusion notes worried her in week six matched her end-of-term concerns. The students whose textured notes suggested hidden strength improved their essays once she targeted office hours at the revision process. She did not change anyone's grade. She changed her teaching.

*(This worked example is a composite drawn from published formative-assessment implementations of Y4-style confidence elicitation and Y5-lite-style confusion notes. The full seven-component GLP framework does not yet have published field-deployment cases at scale. The individual components have documented deployment evidence; the framework as a whole is in its first deployment phase. Maria is plausible. She is not yet citable.)*

## What would change the analysis

The chapter's central practical claim — that two components, well-chosen, produce useful information the artifact alone does not — rests on a generalization from Wiliam-tradition formative-assessment evidence to the specific GLP-component selection. The individual components have documented deployment evidence in K-12 and higher education. The specific combination of GLP-component signals has not been validated at field-study scale. A study showing that two-component implementations produced no actionable information beyond the artifact — that the profiles either agreed with artifact judgments (adding nothing) or disagreed in non-systematic ways (noise) — would collapse the chapter's implementation case.

The ethics section claims observation-for-learning-support and observation-for-surveillance are distinguishable in practice. Selwyn's critique is that they drift toward each other regardless of designer intent. If a longitudinal study showed the drift was not reliably mitigable — that observation-for-learning consistently became observation-for-grading or discipline within a few terms even when designers explicitly resisted it — the chapter's ethical framing would need to be more pessimistic. The honest reading of the existing learning-analytics literature is that the drift is real. The chapter argues mitigation is possible. It does not claim mitigation has been systematically demonstrated at scale.

---

## Still puzzling

Whether one well-implemented component captures most of the first-term diagnostic value, making the two-component recommendation unnecessary complexity, is an open empirical question. The recommendation is a bet on motivation and instrumentation overhead, not on validated signal strength.

Whether the implementation pattern — collect data first term, act on it second term — survives institutional pressure is unclear. Department chairs and accreditors want results quickly. The discipline of holding data without acting on it for a term runs directly against how most institutional reform cycles operate. The chapter is recommending a practice that institutional pressures actively undermine. No implementation guidance the chapter can offer fully resolves this tension.

The framework has been written from the instructor's perspective. Students themselves, especially adult learners, have important things to say about being observed in process. The framework's design has not yet absorbed enough student-voice input. Future versions need to.

The framework's portability to disciplines beyond STEM, writing, and clinical education — art, music, vocational training, physical education — is plausible but unvalidated. The accessibility ladder and context-specific starting points offered here are extrapolations from the documented cases. They may not land cleanly in disciplines whose cognitive-work structure differs substantially from the book's primary examples.

## LLM Exercises

**1. Generate and examine.** Ask an LLM to generate a one-paragraph student confusion note about a concept in your course — first in the "borrowed certainty" register (generic, content-free, high fluency), then in the "genuine engagement" register (specific confusion anchored to an attempted task). Apply the Y5-lite reading to both. Which specific words or phrases in the genuine version could not have been generated without the student actually attempting the task? Use this as calibration for what you are looking for when you read real confusion notes.

**2. Apply to known context.** For your primary teaching context, work through the accessibility ladder and identify your two starting components. For each, write: the exact question, prompt, or protocol you will add; the infrastructure required (name the specific gradebook column, submission field, or question-bank tag); and the one thing you will do differently based on what you observe. If you cannot name the one different action, the component is not yet fully specified — go back and make it concrete.

**3. Stress-test the ethics.** The chapter draws a line between observation for learning support and observation for surveillance. Construct the strongest case that the line is not maintainable in practice: describe a realistic institutional scenario where observation-for-learning drifts into observation-for-discipline within two years, despite the instructor's good intentions. What structural features of the institution make the drift hard to prevent? What would the framework need to add — in its design, its data-ownership rules, or its transparency requirements — to make the drift less likely?

**4. Draft a professional deliverable.** Write the one-page first-term implementation plan described in Exercise 1 of the source chapter's exercise set: name your two components, name the specific implementation for each, name the infrastructure required, and name what you will do differently based on what you observe. Then write the two-sentence explanation from Exercise 2: for a skeptical colleague who trusts artifact-based assessment, what are the two sentences that name the problem and name the response, specifically enough that her first question is "what kinds of traces?" Write three drafts of the two sentences. Keep the best one.
