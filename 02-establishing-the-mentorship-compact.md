# Module 1 — Establishing the Mentorship Compact

**Purpose of this module:** In the first 48 hours of a six-month internship, mentor and intern can lock in incompatible assumptions about timeline, communication, and documentation — and neither one finds out until Week 6, when the resentment surfaces and the data is already suspect. This module gives you the tools to prevent that outcome.

**Learning objectives**

- *(Apply)* Design a Mentorship Compact that specifies the timeline, communication protocol, and ELN documentation standards for a six-month undergraduate internship.
- *(Evaluate)* Scope-check an intern's proposed project against a realistic six-month deliverable horizon and redirect it without crushing motivation.

## The Compact as a Working Specification

Consider a common situation. By Week 6 of a six-month internship, the intern is frustrated because the mentor "never meets with her," and the mentor is frustrated because she "keeps interrupting." Both perceptions are accurate. The mentor understood "regular check-ins" as a brief hallway conversation when their paths crossed; the intern heard "regular check-ins" as a scheduled weekly meeting with an agenda. No one wrote it down, so the gap stayed invisible until it became a source of friction.

The solution draws on something most lab mentors already do in their research: writing a specification before starting a project. A **Mentorship Compact** applies that same practice to the working relationship. It is a written, co-drafted statement of expectations covering timeline, communication, and documentation. The canonical model is the AAMC *Compact Between Biomedical Graduate Students and Their Research Advisors* (2017), which frames the relationship as reciprocal commitments and describes it as a "living document" meant to initiate discussion, not as a legal instrument.

That distinction matters. The Compact is not binding, and it is not an HR formality. Its value is making implicit assumptions explicit before they create problems. The fix for the Week 6 conflict is a single written line: "We meet Tuesdays 2:00–2:30; intern brings three bullet points." Writing the specification prevents the conflict from occurring.

## The Timeline as a Budget

A useful default allocation for a six-month wet-lab internship is **two months training, three months data generation, one month synthesis**. We can treat this as a reasonable starting budget, not a fixed rule — adjust it to the intern and the project.

The training block is the part most likely to be compressed, and the part most important to protect. Undergraduates arrive without the tacit skills that make protocols actually work, and technical competence is the prerequisite for trustworthy data. Compressing training does not save time; it moves the cost downstream, where it appears as protocol drift and unrecoverable data. A mentor who cuts training to three weeks in order to "start collecting early" may find by Month 4 that a cell-culture contamination problem has rendered three months of data uninterpretable. The time lost far exceeds the time saved.

The synthesis month is the other part most commonly cut. An internship that generates data but ends with no analysis, no figures, and no handover has not fulfilled its purpose regardless of what happened at the bench. Budget the final month for synthesis on Day 1, and it will be there when you need it.

## The Communication Handshake: Channel, Latency, and Escalation

"Maintaining effective communication" is one of the named competencies in the CIMER / *Entering Mentoring* mentor-training framework (Pfund, Branchaw & Handelsman). The **Communication Handshake** makes that competency concrete with three specific agreements:

1. **Channel** — what Slack is for (same-day quick questions), what email is for (anything that needs a record), what the bench notebook is for.
2. **Response-latency SOP** — an explicit turnaround expectation, treated like a service-level agreement: "email within 24 hours on weekdays; nothing expected on weekends."
3. **Escalation path** — what the intern does when you are unreachable and something is time-sensitive: "if a reaction is failing and I haven't replied in 30 minutes, ask the lab manager."

Undergraduates often have no frame of reference for what response time is normal, which means silence can read as either neglect or as evidence that they are bothering you — both of which are damaging to the relationship. Being "always available" may seem like the supportive choice, but undefined availability creates anxiety and encourages over-dependence. A defined latency is more supportive than vague openness. An intern who hits an ambiguous result on a Friday afternoon, hears nothing until Monday, and spends the weekend uncertain whether she damaged something will benefit greatly from a one-line escalation rule that converts that uncertainty into an action plan.

## Documentation Standards as a Data-Integrity Issue

A complete electronic lab notebook (ELN) entry is not administrative paperwork — it is the foundation of reproducibility. The governing framework is **ALCOA+**: data must be **A**ttributable, **L**egible, **C**ontemporaneous, **O**riginal, and **A**ccurate, plus **C**omplete (including failed runs), **C**onsistent, **E**nduring, and **A**vailable. The complementary **FAIR** principles — Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable — govern whether a successor can pick the work up, which is exactly what the Month 6 handover depends on.

For undergraduates, the highest-leverage principle is **Contemporaneous**: entries written days after the fact are reconstructions, and reconstructions silently introduce errors. Your job as mentor is to define "complete" as a set of observable criteria, not a general expectation. A complete entry includes the date, the aim, the exact protocol version and any deviations, reagent lot numbers, the location of raw output files, and a one-line interpretation. An entry that reads "ran PCR, worked" — with no annealing temperature, no lot numbers, no gel-image filename — is not usable data. When an anomalous band appears three weeks later, the conditions cannot be reconstructed, and the experiment is effectively gone.

### Worked Example

Your intern proposes learning **four new imaging techniques in Month 1**. The right response is neither immediate approval nor flat refusal. We can apply a three-move scope-check:

1. **Acknowledge the ambition.** "I love that you want range — that instinct is exactly right." This takes her seriously and protects her motivation.
2. **Surface the concrete risk in her terms.** "Here's the issue: four techniques in a month means you'll be mediocre at all four, and mediocre technique produces data we have to throw away."
3. **Redirect to a bounded goal with mastery benchmarks.** "Let's make you genuinely good at confocal first — here's what 'good' looks like [specific benchmarks] — then we add the next one from a position of strength."

Notice that the standard was not lowered. It was raised: mastery over coverage. The narrowing reads as a path to excellence, which is why it builds self-efficacy instead of deflating it.

> **Key Terms**
>
> - **Mentorship Compact** — a co-drafted written specification of timeline, communication, and documentation expectations; a living document, not a binding contract.
> - **2/3/1 timeline** — a default six-month budget: two months training, three months data, one month synthesis.
> - **Communication Handshake** — agreed channel + response-latency SOP + escalation path.
> - **ALCOA+** — the data-integrity standard defining a "complete" ELN entry (Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate, plus Complete/Consistent/Enduring/Available).
> - **Scope-check** — acknowledge ambition → surface the risk → redirect to one bounded goal with mastery benchmarks.

## Module 1 Quiz

1. **(Multiple Choice · Apply)** Your first-semester intern proposes learning four imaging techniques in her first month. What is the best response?
   - A) Approve it — ambition should be rewarded.
   - B) Scope-check: acknowledge the ambition, name the risk of shallow mastery, and prioritize one technique with mastery benchmarks.
   - C) Tell her undergraduates can't handle imaging and assign her pipetting practice.
   - D) Let her try all four and cut the ones that aren't working by Month 2.
   - **Correct: B.** Breadth buys shallow mastery, and shallow mastery produces untrustworthy data; redirecting to one technique with benchmarks raises the standard while protecting motivation.

2. **(True/False · Evaluate)** A Mentorship Compact is a legally binding contract between mentor and intern.
   - **Correct: False.** The Compact is a co-drafted living document whose value is making implicit assumptions explicit; it carries no legal force.

3. **(Scenario · Apply)** You batch email and reply within 24 hours. Your intern Slacks an urgent reagent question, hears nothing for an hour, improvises a substitution, and ruins the run. Which Compact element should have prevented this?
   - A) The 2/3/1 timeline.
   - B) The Communication Handshake (channel + latency SOP + escalation path).
   - C) The ELN documentation standard.
   - D) The scope-check.
   - **Correct: B.** An explicit channel-and-latency norm plus an escalation path tells the intern what response time to expect and what to do when you're unreachable.

4. **(Analyze)** By Week 3, your intern's ELN entries read "split cells, looked fine," while you expected passage number, split ratio, media lot, and confluency. She isn't negligent. Which Compact element failed?
   - A) The communication latency SOP.
   - B) The escalation path.
   - C) The Documentation Standard — "complete" was never defined as observable criteria.
   - D) The synthesis-month allocation.
   - **Correct: C.** No one specified what a complete entry contains, so the gap is a Compact failure, not an intern failure.

5. **(Evaluate)** Rank these six-month scopes by feasibility for a first-semester undergraduate, most feasible first: (i) master one assay and generate a clean dataset; (ii) learn four imaging techniques and publish; (iii) optimize one protocol and characterize three conditions.
   - **Correct: i → iii → ii.** A single mastered assay fits the training-first budget; one optimization plus a few conditions is ambitious but bounded; four techniques plus publication ignores the training block and guarantees shallow mastery.
