# Introduction: Mentorship as a Design Problem

It is Wednesday of a new undergraduate's first week in the lab. She arrived Monday with a printed copy of the group's latest paper, annotated in three colors. Right now she is standing at the bench beside the postdoc who was asked to "show her the ropes," nodding through a pipetting demonstration she is too nervous to interrupt with a question. Her nominal mentor is two doors away, against a grant deadline. Nobody has told her when she will meet with him, what she should be writing in her notebook, or what a good first month looks like. She does not know these things are unsettled; she assumes they were decided long ago and simply not explained to her yet. Six months from now, whether this internship produces a researcher or a quiet disappointment will trace back, more often than not, to this unexamined week.

That is the gap this guide addresses: nearly everyone who supervises undergraduate researchers was trained to do science, and almost no one was trained to do this.

The central idea — the argument you are free to push against — is that undergraduate research failure is a mentor design problem, not a student competence problem. The most common ways these internships go wrong — mismatched expectations, technical drift, stalled identity development, a disorganized handover, quiet exclusion, and prolonged dependence — each results from a missing structure, not a deficient student. Because each failure has a specific cause, each has a fix that can be put in place in advance.

This reframing matters because the natural instinct points in the other direction. When an intern fails a protocol repeatedly, it is easy to assume she is not grasping the technique. When she stops asking questions, it can seem like disengagement. This course trains you to read those same signals as diagnostic information about your own supervision approach — and to respond accordingly. Supervision is a technical skill. It can be specified, practiced, and refined like any other protocol in your lab.

### Who This Is For

This course is designed first for PhD students and postdocs with real domain expertise and no formal training in mentorship — if you have just been assigned your first undergraduate intern and want to mentor effectively without it taking over your year, this course is for you. It is equally useful to faculty who supervise undergraduates directly or who delegate that supervision and want to know what they are delegating. I write from the perspective of someone who has run both sides of that arrangement for decades: as PI and director of the NIH CaNCURE program, which places undergraduates in cancer-nanomedicine research co-ops, and of the Nanomedicine Academy, I have watched hundreds of undergraduate research experiences succeed and fail across many labs — enough to see that the outcomes follow the structures, not the students. The course can be completed in a single sitting before the intern's first day, and individual modules can be revisited as each stage of the internship arrives.

### What This Guide Is, and Is Not

This is a practical handbook: for each predictable failure, a named principle, a lab-situated example, and a practice you can apply on your intern's next working day. It is brief on theory; where it draws on the research literature — cognitive apprenticeship, psychological safety, science-identity formation — the source is named so you can go deeper if you choose.

It is not a treatise on mentoring theory, not a policy manual for your institution's compliance office, and not a guide to supervising graduate students or postdocs, whose timelines and stakes differ in kind. It assumes you already know your science; it adds only the supervision layer.

### The Compact, Throughout

One idea recurs through every module and gives this book its title: the **mentorship compact** — the practice of making mutual expectations explicit, in writing, before events test them. Module 1 builds the literal document, but the compact is better understood as a habit than as a form: every later module is the same move applied to a new domain. Scaffolding is a compact about how training proceeds; the no-fault error policy is a compact about how mistakes are reported; independence benchmarks are a compact about how trust is earned; the exit interview is a compact about how the experience ends. When in doubt anywhere in this course, the answer is usually: make the implicit explicit, together.

### How This Course Is Organized

The six modules follow the intern's lifecycle. Each module addresses a specific failure mode.

**Module 1 — Establishing the Mentorship Compact** (Chapter 2) addresses the Day-1 failure of expectation mismatch. You will design a written compact specifying timeline, communication, and documentation before the intern arrives.

**Module 2 — Scaffolding Technical Competence** (Chapter 3) addresses the Week 1–8 failure of protocol drift. You will execute the three-stage Modeling → Scaffolding → Fading apprenticeship and learn to diagnose why a protocol keeps failing.

**Module 3 — Cultivating Research Identity** (Chapter 4) addresses the Month 2–4 failure of identity stall. You will use psychological safety and intellectual inclusion to move a mentee from lab hand to researcher.

**Module 4 — Transitioning to Independence and Exit** (Chapter 5) addresses the Month 5–6 failure of the botched handover. You will run a structured exit interview and a clean documentation handover. This module ends with the summative exam required for course completion.

**Module 5 — Establishing a Practice of Inclusion** (Chapter 6) and **Module 6 — Fostering Independence** (Chapter 7) are cross-arc practices. They do not belong to a single stage; they apply across the whole internship and across every intern you will ever mentor. Module 5 trains you to recognize and respond to exclusion; Module 6 trains you to define independence benchmarks and exit your own role as the bottleneck.

### How to Use This Course

Read Modules 1–4 in order — each builds on the last, and Module 4's exam assumes familiarity with all of them. Treat Modules 5 and 6 as the practices that separate a successful first internship from a mentorship approach you can repeat. Throughout, every concept is presented the same way: the failure it prevents, the principle, a lab-situated example, and a practice you can apply on your intern's next working day.

### A Note About AI

Your intern arrives carrying an AI assistant, and so do you. It is worth being precise about what that changes in mentorship — and what it cannot.

AI is genuinely useful for the mechanical layers of research training. It can triage a literature: summarize twenty abstracts, surface the three papers worth reading closely, draft the first pass of a protocol summary or a figure legend. Used that way, it buys back hours that mentor and intern can spend on the parts that matter. I encourage interns to use it for exactly that — with the standing rule that anything an AI produces gets verified against the primary source before it enters the notebook, because a fabricated citation in an ELN is a data-integrity failure like any other.

What AI cannot supply is the substance of this book. It cannot confer recognition — the moment a mentor says "that's a researcher's question" and means it. It cannot model tacit technique at the bench, narrate the reasoning behind a protocol step, or make it safe to report a broken objective within the hour. And it cannot form judgment. An intern who asks an AI what her ambiguous gel means gets an answer; an intern who is asked by her mentor "what do *you* think it means?" gets a research identity. The difference between those two events is the entire subject of Module 3. Literature triage can be delegated to a machine. Judgment formation is built in conversation, error by error, interpretation by interpretation, between two people who trust each other — which is to say, it is built inside the compact. So treat AI use as one more expectation to make explicit on day one: name what the intern may delegate to it, what must be verified, and what she is expected to attempt herself first. That is a compact like any other in this book. Let AI do the summarizing. The mentoring is yours.
