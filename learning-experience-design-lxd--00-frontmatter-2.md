## Preface

Somewhere right now, an edtech company is shipping an AI tutor. The product designer on the team is excellent. The interface is clean, the response latency is low, the AI is patient and articulate. Session ratings are strong, retention curves point the right way, and the dashboard the leadership team reviews every Monday shows engagement climbing. Nobody in the building has read Bjork. Nobody has run a delayed retention test. Nobody has asked what happens on the exam when the app is closed. This is not negligence in any individual sense — it is the normal condition. Google's own engineers, documenting the development of their LearnLM models, acknowledged that standard frontier models do not naturally align with pedagogical goals: they default to delivering answers, fail to manage cognitive load, and require deliberate, separate engineering work to support learning rather than simply satisfy the user. Most products never commission that work. This book is about what a person trained in learning experience design does differently in that building.

The question is urgent now for a structural reason. Generative AI has collapsed the cost of building something that looks like a learning experience. An adaptive tutor, a personalized curriculum, a Socratic dialogue system — each of these once required a team and a year; each can now be assembled by one developer in a weekend. The people doing the assembling are overwhelmingly trained in product design, software engineering, and growth — disciplines whose optimization targets are engagement, retention, and daily active use. Those targets are not wrong; they are simply not learning, and nothing in the standard product toolkit can tell the difference. Meanwhile, the dominant way learners actually encounter AI is not a pedagogically designed system at all: surveys find that the great majority of students — 86% in the Digital Education Council's global survey — already use AI in their studies, mostly through generic consumer chat apps built to be maximally helpful in the moment. The gap between what those products optimize for and what learning science says produces durable capability is the reason this discipline exists.

The book's thesis follows from that gap: pedagogical design, not technological capability, determines whether AI-mediated education produces learning. The evidence base now contains results that look contradictory until you see this variable. A randomized trial found that high school students with access to a generic GPT interface got dramatically better at practice problems and measurably worse on the unassisted exam. Another randomized trial found that a carefully engineered AI tutor outperformed expert-led active learning at Harvard. These are not contradictions. One system was an answer machine; the other was built around seven specific principles from cognitive psychology. The delivery channel was the same. The design was not. The variable that matters is the one this book teaches you to control.

A note on the evidence standard, because a book that argues for measurement discipline must practice it. Every design recommendation in these pages is an empirical claim, and empirical claims have evidence levels: randomized trials are labeled as such, preprints are flagged as preprints, contested findings are labeled contested, and industry reports are not dressed up as science. The book maintains an open ledger of its sources for a reason that the field recently demonstrated on our behalf: a widely cited study on ChatGPT's effect on learning performance — Wang and Fan (2025) — was retracted in April 2026, after it had already propagated through the literature. Evidence rots. A discipline that builds on evidence needs a disclosure habit that lets it find the rot. You will see that habit, called the Evidence Disclosure, throughout this book. It is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a field and a fashion.
