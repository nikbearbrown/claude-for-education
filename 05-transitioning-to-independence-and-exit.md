# Module 4 — Transitioning to Independence and Exit

**Purpose of this module:** By month 6, if the raw data is spread across three notebooks and a shared-drive folder named "misc," the samples are labeled with personal abbreviations only the intern can decode, and no exit conversation has taken place, six months of data can become functionally lost — unfindable, unreproducible — and the intern leaves with no career conversation and no basis for a strong recommendation letter. The science may have been fine. The internship still fails its institutional function. This module gives you the tools to close the arc properly.

**Learning objectives**
- *(Apply)* Conduct a structured exit interview — growth review, career discussion, network conversation — and complete a documentation handover a successor can actually use.
- *(Analyze)* Synthesize mentorship decisions across Modules 1–4, reading the exit as the close of a single coherent arc.

## The Exit Interview

The exit interview is not a performance review. The intern is not an employee being evaluated, and a grading frame invites defensiveness and closes down the candid reflection the meeting is meant to produce. Any evaluative notes belong in private records that inform a recommendation letter — not in this conversation.

The exit interview is a developmental conversation in three parts: **growth review → career discussion → network conversation**. The orientation is forward. You are running a launch briefing, not a grade report. (The undergraduate-specific exit-interview literature is thin; treat this structure as synthesized best practice, not a validated protocol — but it is built from solid career-mentoring frameworks and offboarding guidance.) <!-- FACT-CHECK FLAG: UNVERIFIED — see factchecks/05-transitioning-to-independence-and-exit-assertions.md (growth → career → network three-movement structure is the course's own synthesis, as the prose itself states) -->

The **growth review** names a concrete arc: "In March you were running the protocol I handed you. By May you were proposing the controls and catching anomalies before I did." That arc is the Module 3 transition from lab hand to researcher, told back to her as a story she can carry. The **career discussion** comes next (below). The **network conversation** closes it: who you can introduce her to, what doors you can open.

**Practice:** Open the exit interview by naming her growth arc, not by evaluating her performance.

## Documentation Handover

The operational test is one question: could a new lab member pick up this work without the intern present? That is the FAIR standard (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) applied at the bench. This is not administrative tidiness — it is the same data-integrity obligation Module 1 set for ELN entries, and there is a governing fact behind it: under standard data-ownership policy (echoing the AAMC Compact, 2017), the notebooks, files, and physical samples belong to the institution and stay in the lab so others can reproduce and continue the work. The intern does not own the data she generated; handing it over in usable form is an obligation, not a favor.

The standard for sample labels is successor-readability, not author-readability. "J1–J40" is uninterpretable in three months. Date + initials + project code + condition, plus a key in the ELN, makes the box usable by anyone.

![Same freezer box with personal shorthand labels blocking successor access versus four-field labels enabling it](../images/05-transitioning-to-independence-and-exit-fig-01.png)
*Figure 5.1 — Samples labeled in personal shorthand are uninterpretable to any successor, while labels carrying date, initials, project code, and condition — plus a key in the ELN — make the same box usable by anyone.*

**Practice:** Nothing should leave the freezer or the drive under personal shorthand only the intern can decode.

## Career Trajectory Conversation

A second- or third-year PhD student has more to offer here than you might expect. Whether the intern is leaning toward medical school, a PhD, or industry, you can deliver three things: a structured goals conversation, a network introduction, and a recommendation letter.

Use an Individual Development Plan (IDP / myIDP) lens — self-assessment, exploration, goals, next steps — scaled down for a six-month intern (the full myIDP is heavier than an undergraduate needs). Identify where her strongest gains actually were ("your biggest growth was in experimental design, not just technique"), then match the offer to her path: an MD-PhD introduction for the medical-school-curious, a PI contact for the PhD-bound, an alum in industry for the applied track.

On the **recommendation letter** — the most durable artifact you will produce — two evidence-based rules apply. First, specificity over adjectives: "independently troubleshot a failing qPCR, proposed the contamination hypothesis that proved correct, and presented it at lab meeting" is far more effective than "hardworking and pleasant." Second, guard against gendered language: women are disproportionately described in communal terms (helpful, warm, team player) and men in agentic ones (independent, intellectual, leader), and communal-only letters read as faint praise and encode bias. Foreground research competence and agentic accomplishment for every mentee.

**Practice:** Frame the letter around specific, agentic research accomplishments — concrete things she did, not traits she has.

## The Final Two Months as Synthesis

Months 5–6 are for synthesis, not new data. A new experimental line started in month 5 will not finish cleanly before the intern leaves — it becomes exactly the orphaned, half-documented data that creates the problem described above, and it crowds out the handover and reflection that give the internship lasting value. Redirect new-experiment energy into writing up completed work as figure-ready datasets, presenting at lab meeting, and building the successor handover. That synthesis is the productive output of those weeks: it converts scattered results into a reproducible record and into her own research narrative.

### Worked Example

You sit down with your intern for the exit interview. Two weeks left.

**Opening framing:** "This isn't a review of how you did — it's about how far you've come and where it points. Let's start there." This reframes the conversation away from evaluation.

**Growth review:** "In March I was handing you protocols. By May you flagged that dilution error before I saw it and argued for the contamination hypothesis — which was right. You stopped executing and started owning the work." Then you ask: "Where did you grow the most? Where did you struggle?"

**The undisclosed Month-3 struggle:** She pauses, then says, "Honestly — in March I was completely lost. I felt like I didn't belong here and I almost asked to leave. I never told you." The wrong responses are reflexive apology or relitigating March. The right response is to receive it without defensiveness, thank her for the honesty, and treat it as information about your lab's psychological safety in month 3 — she did not feel safe enough to report it, which is a Module 3 signal. "Thank you for telling me. I wish I'd made it easier to say that in March — that's on me to fix for the next person. What would have helped?" You learn from it and move forward.

**Documentation walk-through:** Together you build the single ELN index, decode and relabel "J1–J40," and draft the one-page successor handoff.

**Career conversation:** She is leaning toward medical school but liked the research. You frame her growth around experimental design, introduce her to an MD-PhD down the hall, and confirm that the letter will lead with the qPCR troubleshoot, not "delightful to have in lab."

> **Documentation Handover Checklist**
>
> *Copy this. Walk it with the intern in the final two weeks.*
>
> **Data**
> - [ ] Single ELN index built, listing every dataset and exactly where it lives
> - [ ] "misc" / scratch folders cleared, contents relabeled and filed, or deleted
> - [ ] Every raw file named to lab convention (date_project_condition_replicate)
> - [ ] Every figure or result linked back to the raw data and the ELN entry that produced it
> - [ ] All personal abbreviations and shorthand decoded in a key in the ELN
> - [ ] Notebooks/ELN shared with the PI; access confirmed not tied to the intern's account
>
> **Physical samples & reagents**
> - [ ] Every tube, plate, slide, and cell line labeled: date + initials + project code + condition
> - [ ] Freezer/box map updated; location of every sample findable without the intern
> - [ ] Samples to keep transferred to a named owner; samples to discard properly disposed of
> - [ ] Custom reagents/stocks labeled with prep date, concentration, and maker
>
> **Handoff & close**
> - [ ] One-page successor handoff: project state, what's done, what's next, known issues
> - [ ] Successor test passed: a lab member who wasn't involved can locate and interpret one dataset cold
> - [ ] Completed work written up / presented (synthesis, not new data)

> **Key Terms**
> - **Exit interview:** a forward-looking developmental conversation (growth → career → network), not a performance review.
> - **FAIR / "complete for a successor":** Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable; the test is whether a successor could reproduce the work from the freezer and the ELN alone.
> - **Individual Development Plan (IDP / myIDP):** a structured career tool — self-assessment, exploration, goals, next steps — scaled down for a six-month intern.
> - **Agentic vs. communal letter language:** specific, accomplishment-based ("independently optimized…") rather than trait-based ("warm, helpful"); guards against gender bias.
> - **Data ownership:** notebooks, files, and samples belong to the institution and stay in the lab (echoing the AAMC Compact, 2017); the handover is an obligation, not a courtesy.

## Module 4 — Final Summative Exam
*(Required for course completion.)*

**1. (Multiple Choice — M4)** The single best test of whether a documentation handover is "complete" is:
- A. The intern can still find everything if she returns.
- B. The ELN entries are filled in for every experiment.
- C. A lab member who was never involved can locate and interpret the work without the intern present.
- D. The PI has signed off on the final report.

**Correct: C.** The FAIR standard is successor-readability, not author-readability — completeness means a stranger can pick it up cold.

**2. (True/False — M1 + M4)** Because the Mentorship Compact and the exit handover are both internal lab agreements, the intern retains ownership of the data and samples she generated.

**Correct: False.** Under standard data-ownership policy (echoing the AAMC Compact), notebooks, files, and samples belong to the institution and stay in the lab. The Compact (M1) sets documentation as a data-integrity obligation; the handover (M4) enforces it at exit.

**3. (Scenario — M2 + M4)** In the exit interview, your intern reveals that back in week 5 she failed a PCR three times, quietly tweaked the protocol on her own to "make it work," and never told you. The data from that period is in the dataset you're about to archive. What two things does this require you to do?

**Correct:** (1) **Data integrity:** flag and re-examine that period's data before archiving — an undocumented protocol deviation means those results may not be reproducible or trustworthy; note the deviation in the handover. (2) **Diagnosis (M2):** this is a co-observation/scaffolding gap *and* a psychological-safety gap — she didn't feel safe reporting repeated failure, so she hid it. Treat it as a lab-climate signal, not a character flaw.

**4. (Apply — M3 + M4)** Rewrite this recommendation sentence to be specific and agentic: *"Maria was a pleasure to work with and always willing to help her labmates."*

**Correct (model answer):** "Maria independently diagnosed a contamination problem in our cell culture, proposed and tested the fix that halved our variability, and presented the result at lab meeting." Rationale: foregrounds a concrete, agentic research accomplishment over communal traits — predicts better and guards against gendered "faint praise."

**5. (Multiple Choice — M4)** Your intern wants to start a new knockout line in month 5. The best response is to:
- A. Encourage it — more data strengthens her CV.
- B. Redirect to writing up the completed line, presenting it, and building the handover.
- C. Let her start it but plan to finish it yourself after she leaves.
- D. Decline and assign her routine maintenance for the final weeks.

**Correct: B.** Months 5–6 are for synthesis; a new line won't finish cleanly and becomes orphaned, half-documented data. Synthesis is the productive output of those weeks.

**6. (Synthesis — M1 + M2 + M3)** A mentor complains: "My intern follows every protocol perfectly but never volunteers an interpretation, and I just found out she hid a broken reagent for a week." Name the *one* underlying condition most likely driving *both* behaviors, and the prior-module practice that most directly addresses it.

**Correct:** The underlying condition is **low psychological safety** (M3) — the silence on interpretation and the hidden error are the same withholding behavior under different names. The most direct fix is the **No-Fault error policy** stated explicitly plus **intellectual inclusion** (ask "what do you think is happening?" before supplying the answer). A correct answer must connect *both* symptoms to the single safety/recognition root, not treat them as two unrelated problems.

**7. (Synthesis — M1 + M2 + M3 + M4)** Put these four mentor instruments in the order of the intern lifecycle, and state in one phrase what failure each prevents: exit handover, Mentorship Compact, No-Fault error policy, three-stage scaffold.

**Correct:** (1) **Mentorship Compact** (day 1–2) — prevents expectation mismatch; (2) **Three-stage scaffold / Modeling→Scaffolding→Fading** (weeks 1 through months) — prevents protocol drift; (3) **No-Fault error policy** (months 2–4) — prevents the identity/safety stall and unreported, compounding errors; (4) **Exit handover** (months 5–6) — prevents the botched, unreproducible departure. Each maps to its lifecycle failure mode.

**8. (Analyze — M2 + M6 preview)** Two interns ask you the identical question: *"Should I run the positive control?"* One is in week 4 (early Scaffolding); one is in month 5 (late Fading). Why should your response differ?

**Correct:** Same words, different developmental meaning. For the **week-4 intern** (Scaffolding), the question is a genuine knowledge gap — answer it and explain *why* the control matters. For the **month-5 intern** (Fading), answering it directly reinforces dependence; instead ask "what do you think, and why?" — she should already own that judgment, and supplying the answer makes you the bottleneck. The mentor action is keyed to the scaffolding stage, not the literal question.

**9. (Extended Scenario, multi-part — full 6-month arc, M1–M4)** You onboard an intern in January. Walk the arc:
- **(a)** She proposes mastering four imaging techniques in month 1. What do you do, and which Compact element governs it?
- **(b)** In week 5 she fails the same protocol three times despite following the written SOP exactly. What is your diagnostic move?
- **(c)** By week 8 she's technically flawless but never interprets her own data. What is the single most effective change to your data-review habit?
- **(d)** In June, with two weeks left, the data is in a "misc" folder and samples are in personal shorthand. Name the first deliverable you build.

**Correct:** (a) **Scope-check** — acknowledge the ambition, surface the risk (shallow mastery → bad data), redirect to one technique with mastery benchmarks; governed by the Compact's scope/timeline element. (b) **Co-observe** her technique to find the unwritten habit gap (pipetting, contamination, reagent handling) the written SOP doesn't capture — distinguish SOP gap from habit gap. (c) **Ask for her interpretation before offering yours** — "what do you think is happening?" not "did you understand?" (intellectual inclusion / recognition). (d) The **single ELN index** mapping every dataset's location, then relabel samples and write the one-page successor handoff. A full-credit answer shows each stage's instrument is distinct and lifecycle-appropriate.

**10. (Evaluate — M3 + M4 synthesis)** During the exit interview your intern discloses that she felt lost and almost quit in month 3 but said nothing. Another mentor argues this proves she "wasn't really ready for research." Evaluate that claim.

**Correct:** Reject it. The disclosure is evidence about the **lab's psychological safety in month 3** (M3), not about her readiness — she was competent enough to stay and grow, but did not feel safe enough to report distress. The correct read treats it as a climate signal pointing back at the mentor ("what would have surfaced this sooner?"), consistent with the exit interview's developmental, non-evaluative frame. Full credit requires naming both that the claim misattributes a climate problem to the intern *and* that the developmental (not performance) framing of the exit is what makes the disclosure useful.
