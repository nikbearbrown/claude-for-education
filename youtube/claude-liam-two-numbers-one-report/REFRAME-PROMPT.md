## THE NUMBERS — verified live, use these exactly

### Pick 1 — the platform (Substack)

Chris Best's announcement quotes one figure from Pangram's social-media report:

> "as much as 40% on some platforms, according to Pangram's estimate"

That report contains four numbers. All true, all the same dataset:

| figure | what it measures |
|---|---|
| **13.8%** | average AI rate across all scanned items, all platforms |
| **21.9%** | **Substack's** own combined AI-generated + AI-assisted rate |
| **25.72%** | longform items over 250 words, all platforms, fully AI |
| **40%+** | **LinkedIn** longform, fully AI — the number that got quoted |

The two figures that describe Substack — 13.8% global average and 21.9% for Substack itself — were not the ones quoted. The report's own sentence is: *"Substack, which was the longform platform with the lowest combined AI rate, still saw more than a fifth of its posts (21.9%) flag as AI-generated or AI-assisted."* And earlier: *"Substack was an exception."*

So: a feature shipping on Substack is motivated by LinkedIn's number, from a report that singles Substack out as the least affected longform platform.

**IMPORTANT — do not invert this.** The pick is **40% instead of 13.8% or 21.9%**. Best quoted HIGH. Any phrasing that implies he quoted 13.8% to downplay is wrong and must not reach a beat.

### Pick 2 — the vendor (Pangram)

The vendor's evaluations page quotes 97.5% and the zero-false-positive result. It does not quote 65%. The pre-2019, non-native-speaker composition of the human corpus does not appear either.

### The mathiness layer

Every number in this film carries a decimal: 13.8, 21.9, 25.72, 97.5, 65.0, and Pangram's self-reported **0.01%** false-positive rate. The decimal signals measurement precision. But precision is not the thing under dispute — **selection** is. The decimal point does rhetorical work the measurement never authorised. That is the film's second layer, and it is what makes this more than a "someone quoted the big number" story.

## Using the word "mathiness" — use it bare, attribute it to nobody

Use the word. Do not cite anyone for it.

The attribution is contested and the film cannot afford to litigate it. Wikipedia credits Paul Romer with coining it at the American Economic Association meeting in January 2015, in the paper "Mathiness in the Theory of Economic Growth". At least one academic source says Romer was not first and credits Jordan Ellenberg with earlier use. That earlier attribution could not be confirmed against a primary source.

Two further problems with citing Romer even if he were first:

1. **His sense is narrower than ours.** Romer's subject is formal economic models, where the symbols and the natural-language claims drift apart — mathematics as smokescreen inside a model. This film is about selective quotation of descriptive statistics. Adjacent, not identical. Importing his definition would import a constraint the film then has to explain away.
2. **The word does not need an owner.** "Mathiness" is transparent English — the same construction as "truthiness" — and it means what it sounds like: the appearance of mathematical rigour without the substance. A viewer needs no citation to understand it.

So the rule is: **use "mathiness" as a plain word, with no attribution, no date, no name.** If a beat wants to define it, define it in the film's own words against the film's own evidence — six decimal points doing work the measurement never authorised.

Bolting a contested citation onto a self-explanatory word, inside a film about qualifiers falling off in transit, would be the error the film exists to describe. Do not do it.
