## Preface

Most people who mentor undergraduate researchers have not received formal training in how to do it. A PhD student spends years learning to design experiments, run instruments, and defend results — and is then asked to supervise an undergraduate without any preparation for that role. There is no standard course on mentorship, and most mentors learn as they go.

This book is a practical guide to help with that. It is built on one central idea: when an undergraduate research experience does not go well — when the data is unusable, when the student drifts away, when months of work produce nothing that can be built on — the problem is almost never the student's intelligence or ability. It is a failure of design. The mentor did not set clear expectations, did not build the right kind of support structure, did not make the lab safe enough for the student to report a mistake, or did not plan for the end of the experience. Each of those is a preventable design error, and each has a known fix.

This guide is written as a practical handbook. It is meant to be read once before a student's first day, and then consulted by topic across the months that follow. It is brief on theory and specific about what to do. Where it draws on the research literature — on cognitive apprenticeship, on psychological safety, on how scientific identity forms — the source is named so you can explore it further if you choose. That additional reading is optional. The goal is to give you what you need to mentor well, starting now.
