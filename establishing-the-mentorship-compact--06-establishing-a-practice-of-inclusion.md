# Module 5 — Establishing a Practice of Inclusion

**Purpose of this module:** An intern can be fully included on paper — key, project, Slack access — and still quietly stop participating, and the mentor never sees it because they are tracking permissions instead of behavior. This module addresses that gap.

*(Cross-arc practice — applies across the whole internship, not one stage.)*

**Learning objectives**
- *(Analyze)* Identify exclusionary situations in a laboratory context and diagnose the mentor behavior that created or failed to prevent them.
- *(Evaluate)* Reflect on how diversity — demographic, disciplinary, experiential — changes which mentorship practices work, and adjust your practice accordingly.

## Access Without Belonging

Two interns join the same lab in the same week. Both receive a key, Slack access, and a project. The onboarding is identical. By Week 6, Intern A asks questions in lab meeting and pushes back on an interpretation. Intern B answers when asked but never initiates, routing every question through one postdoc privately. Same access. Different experience.

The difference is what this module addresses. **Inclusion** refers to the set of conditions you can grant: the key, the seat at the meeting, the project assignment. **Belonging** is what the intern actually experiences — the felt sense that her presence is expected and her contributions count. Inclusion is necessary but not sufficient for belonging. It is possible to include someone completely on paper while they continue to feel like an outsider.

Belonging is not a vague internal state that is hard to observe. It shows up in behavior. An intern who does not feel she belongs will stop volunteering interpretations, go quiet in group settings while remaining engaged one-on-one, channel questions through a single trusted person, arrive early and leave promptly to minimize unstructured social exposure, and under-report errors. Intern B is exhibiting all of these behaviors. Your job as mentor is to read that pattern — not to confirm that B has a key.

*Figure 6.1 — Inclusion is the set of conditions a mentor can grant — key, seat, project, channel — while belonging is the intern's experienced participation, observable only in behavior, and the first does not produce the second by itself.*

This matters because belonging is an input to outcomes the lab cares about. In STEM, domain-specific sense of belonging predicts persistence more strongly than general belonging, and the effect is largest for underrepresented and low-income students; science-identity development is itself a strong predictor of who continues to graduate school (NASEM 2019, *The Science of Effective Mentorship in STEMM*, Ch. 3). To know whether an intern belongs, watch participation, not permissions.

## Exclusion Without Intent

An intern from a non-dominant background arrives ten minutes early to lab meeting. Everyone else arrives on time. You open the meeting with an in-joke about a weekend outing she was not part of. She says nothing.

Nothing was malicious. The effect was still exclusionary. The joke activated an informal network she is not part of and marked her as outside the in-group. Both things are true at once: no bad intent, and a real effect on participation. That coexistence is the central point.

Exclusion comes in two forms that require different responses. **Active exclusion** has an identifiable actor and act: a dismissive comment, a question framed as "naive," a public correction, an invitation someone was left off. **Structural exclusion** has no actor — it is built into how the lab routinely operates: the decisions made at a lunch the commuting intern cannot attend, the assumption that everyone has read the foundational paper, the vocabulary that presumes a shared disciplinary background. No one decided to exclude her; the barrier is simply built into the environment.

Three implicit norms do this kind of silent work, and each can be identified by observation:

- **Inside jokes and shared references** index membership in a group the intern is not part of. Indicator: laughter she cannot join; she goes quiet or produces a delayed smile.
- **Informal networks** carry real decisions off the main channel — in hallways, at lunch, in a group chat she is not on. Indicator: she is consistently the last to hear about changes that affect her work.
- **Assumed shared culture or vocabulary** presumes everyone shares the same disciplinary background and terminology. Indicator: her questions get labeled "naive," and she stops asking them.

The approach to avoid is the mentor who responds to every belonging signal by confirming that the intern has formal access ("but she has a key and a project!") and by restating general commitments to diversity — while changing no procedure and reading no behavior. Participation keeps declining; retention stays at 100% and she never advances. That approach confuses inclusion with belonging and substitutes a stated value for an action. Exclusion is defined by its effect on participation, not by anyone's intent — so diagnose it from behavior, not from whether someone meant harm.

## The Three-Response Model: Name It, Redirect It, Invite Explicitly

Once you have identified an exclusionary moment, the next step is a concrete response. This course provides three options, each suited to a different situation. (This triad is the course's own decision tool, not a validated instrument — but its components each have practitioner support in the psychological-safety literature, e.g., Edmondson 1999.)

- **Name it** — make the mechanism briefly explicit, in a low-key way, usually to the group. *"Hang on — that joke's from before Maya started. Maya, the short version is…"* Use this when an active exclusion just occurred in the room and letting it pass would normalize it. Used too frequently, it can feel like policing — reserve it for in-the-moment active exclusions.
- **Redirect it** — reframe without confrontation; return legitimacy to the contribution that was dismissed. *"That's actually the right question — outside molecular bio nobody would assume that."* Use this when a question or idea was dismissed.
- **Invite explicitly** — proactively bring the intern into a conversation or decision she is structurally outside of. *"Maya, before we settle this — what's your read?"* Use this for structural exclusion and the quiet-in-groups pattern.

The decision rule is: active exclusion, just happened, in the room — name it (a membership boundary was drawn) or redirect it (a contribution was dismissed). Structural exclusion, or a recurring quiet-in-groups pattern — invite explicitly and change the procedure. When uncertain, invite explicitly — it has the lowest social cost and addresses the most common case.

![Decision flowchart matching response to exclusion mechanism: active exclusion routes to name-it or redirect-it, structural exclusion routes to invite-explicitly plus change-procedure](../images/06-establishing-a-practice-of-inclusion-fig-02.png)
*Figure 6.2 — An exclusionary moment is diagnosed by mechanism — an active act with an identifiable actor gets named or redirected in the moment, while structural exclusion or a recurring quiet-in-groups pattern gets an explicit invitation plus a procedure change.*

A common misstep is defaulting to "call it out." Naming an active exclusion can be appropriate, but structural exclusion has no specific actor to name — you have to change a procedure. Matching the response to the mechanism is the core skill.

## Diversity as a Design Input

Diversity is most useful when treated not as a goal to affirm but as an input that changes which practices are needed — across three observable dimensions. **Demographic** (race, gender, first-generation status): ignoring identity is itself a design failure, because it leaves identity-related participation barriers unaddressed (NASEM 2019, Ch. 3, on the cost of the "colorblind" stance). **Disciplinary**: an engineering intern in a biochem lab arrives with different vocabulary and different default assumptions. **Experiential**: a first-generation intern with no model of how research labs work needs more implicit norms made explicit.

One misconception worth addressing directly: "treating everyone the same is the fair default." Identical treatment ignores the fact that the engineering intern needs a vocabulary primer the molecular-biology intern does not. Equity here means matching the support to the intern's actual starting point — an observable difference, not an ideological one.

The Mentorship Compact (Module 1) is your inclusion instrument, because it allows you to address structural exclusion in writing before the internship begins. Concrete, behavioral clauses: name the decision channel ("project decisions happen in our Monday 1:1, not at lunch"); state a vocabulary norm ("no question is too basic — that's a rule, not a kindness"); make the no-fault error policy explicit. Build inclusion into procedure before the internship starts, so belonging does not depend on the intern's nerve.

### Worked Example

An engineering-background intern is mentored by a biochemist. The lab's vocabulary assumes molecular biology, and her colleagues keep framing her questions as "naive." Over two weeks, her question rate drops.

Diagnose it by mechanism: this is assumed shared culture (structural exclusion) compounded by active framing ("naive"). The behavioral evidence is the declining question rate — not a complaint, which she is unlikely to file.

Respond by matching the tool to each mechanism. For the active framing, redirect it in the moment: "That's a fair question — most people outside molecular bio wouldn't assume that; here's why we do X." For her standing, invite explicitly: "Your engineering framing is actually why I wanted you on this — keep bringing that lens." For the structural gap, change the procedure: add a one-page vocabulary-and-key-papers primer to onboarding, so the assumed-shared-culture barrier is closed by design rather than by her willingness to keep asking into a room that calls her questions naive.

What you should not do is deliver a private speech about valuing diverse perspectives while leaving the vocabulary gap and the "naive" framing in place. That is a stated value where a behavior is needed.

> **Key Terms**
> - **Inclusion:** structural and procedural access you can grant (key, seat, project, channel).
> - **Belonging:** the intern's experienced, observable state of legitimate participation.
> - **Active exclusion:** an interpersonal act with an identifiable actor.
> - **Structural exclusion:** exclusion built into routine lab procedure, with no actor and no intent required.
> - **Three-response model:** the course's decision tool — *name it / redirect it / invite explicitly* — matched to mechanism.

## Module 5 Quiz

**1. (Scenario)** At lab meeting you open with an inside joke about a weekend outing your newest intern wasn't part of. She goes silent. What is the exclusionary mechanism, and what is the best mentor response?
A. No mechanism — no one intended harm; do nothing.
B. Active exclusion via an inside joke / informal network; *name it* to fold her in, then stop opening meetings with in-group references.
C. Structural exclusion; deliver a lab-wide statement on valuing diversity.
D. A confidence problem in the intern; tell her to speak up more.
**Correct: B.** The joke indexes an informal network she's outside of (active exclusion); a light *name it* repairs the moment, and the structural self-correction prevents the next one. Intent is irrelevant to the mechanism.

**2. (Multiple choice)** In a lab context, what is the difference between inclusion and belonging?
A. They are the same thing measured two ways.
B. Inclusion is access you grant; belonging is participation the intern experiences, read from behavior.
C. Belonging is access; inclusion is feelings.
D. Inclusion is for newcomers; belonging is for senior members.
**Correct: B.** Inclusion is grantable (key, seat, project); belonging is the experienced state, detectable only by watching participation.

**3. (Apply)** An intern stops contributing in group settings but is fully engaged one-on-one. What structural change best addresses this?
A. Wait for her to raise it herself.
B. Re-confirm she has Slack and meeting access.
C. *Invite explicitly* — build a round-robin or a direct prompt ("what's your read?") into group meetings so her contribution doesn't depend on volunteering.
D. Stop holding group meetings.
**Correct: C.** The quiet-in-groups pattern is a structural/belonging signal; explicit invitation addresses the most common case at the lowest social cost. Re-checking access (B) is the colorblind error.

**4. (Analyze)** A lab has a 100% intern retention rate, but no intern from an underrepresented background has ever transitioned to graduate school from it. What does this data most suggest?
A. The lab is succeeding fully; retention is the goal.
B. High retention can mask a belonging deficit that suppresses the downstream outcome (advancement), which the mentor should have tracked behaviorally.
C. The interns simply weren't interested in graduate school.
D. The lab needs a stronger diversity values statement.
**Correct: B.** Retention measures presence; advancement reflects belonging and science-identity development. The gap is the belonging deficit the module warns about — not solvable by a values statement (D).

**5. (Evaluate)** You review a sample Mentorship Compact that specifies the lab Slack channel but says nothing about where project decisions get made. Which modification best strengthens inclusion?
A. Add a paragraph affirming the lab's commitment to diversity.
B. Add a clause moving consequential decisions to a scheduled, announced channel everyone can access, closing the informal-network gap.
C. Remove the Slack clause; it's redundant.
D. Add a rule that the intern must attend all lunches.
**Correct: B.** Naming the decision channel pre-empts structural exclusion via the "we decide at lunch" informal network — a behavioral fix in the Compact, not an aspirational one (A).

***

We now have a behavioral toolkit: read belonging from participation, distinguish active from structural exclusion, and match name-it / redirect-it / invite-explicitly to the mechanism. Belonging is also a precondition for what comes next — an intern who does not feel she belongs will not risk the independent judgment Module 6 asks for. Before you can fade your support, make sure she has a seat, not just a key.
